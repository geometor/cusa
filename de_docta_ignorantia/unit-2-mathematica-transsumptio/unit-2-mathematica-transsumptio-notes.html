Unit II — Incorruptible Certitude & the Three-Step Method of Mathematical Transsumption — Critical Commentary & Notes¶

Exact Symbolic Reconstruction, Latin Philology & Canon Reception · DI I.11–12 (1440)

I. Epistemological Structure of Transsumptio (DI I.11–12)¶

Why does Nicholas of Cusa privilege geometry over every sensible or verbal analogy for divine science? In DI I.11 §2, Cusa locates mathematicalia at the exact epistemological fulcrum between material instability and pure intellectual simplicity:

  1. Incorruptible Certitude: Sensible things waver continually because material possibility abounds in them, whereas mathematical figures—constructed by the mind itself—are nobis certissima (“most certain to us”).

  2. Refutation of Atomism (`DI I.11 §3`): Echoing Aristotle's Physics VI and the Pythagorean discovery of the incommensurability of the square’s diagonal, Cusa notes that mathematical demonstration alone refutes Epicurus’s indivisible magnitude-atoms: if a unit square were composed of \(N\) indivisible atoms per side, its diagonal would require \(N\sqrt{2}\) atoms, contradicting integer indivisibility.

  3. The Three-Tier Operator (`DI I.12 §1`): Let \(\mathcal{F}_R\) denote a one-parameter family of finite geometric figures indexed by scale parameter \(R \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}\), and let \(\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{F}_R)\) be an algebraic invariant (such as curvature, sagitta, or vertex spread):

    • Step 1 (*Consideratio finitorum*): Compute \(\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{F}_R)\) exactly over \(\mathbb{Q}\) or radical extensions for finite \(R\).

    • Step 2 (*Translatio ad infinitum*): Evaluate \(\lim_{R \to \infty} \mathcal{I}(\mathcal{F}_R)\) in intellectual vision, revealing the coincidence of properties that were strictly incompatible for every finite \(R\).

    • Step 3 (*Transsumptio ad simplex absolutum*): Transfer that coincidence beyond spatial extension altogether to the figure-free Absolute Maximum.

II. Historical & Textual Context¶

  • Patristic & Twelfth-Century Antecedents (`DI I.12 §2`): Cusa synthesizes four classic theological symbols: (i) Anselm of Canterbury's De Veritate on supreme truth as rectitudo; (ii) pseudo-Hermetic and Chartrian formulas from the twelfth-century Liber XXIV philosophorum (“Deus est sphaera infinita cuius centrum est ubique, circumferentia nusquam”); (iii) Boethius (De Institutione Arithmetica I.1–2) on multitude and magnitude; and (iv) Augustine's De quantitate animae, which ascends from point, line, triangle, and circle to the incorporeal soul.

  • Kepler’s Tribute: In Chapter II of Mysterium Cosmographicum (kepler:mysterium:1596), Johannes Kepler hailed Cusa (divinus mihi Cusanus) specifically for this transsumptive correlation of the curved and the straight as the supreme mathematical emblem of Creator and creature.
