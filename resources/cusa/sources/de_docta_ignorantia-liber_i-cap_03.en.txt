### Book I, Chapter 3 — That Precise Truth Is Incomprehensible
# Witness: h I, nn. 9–10 · Paris 1514 fol. 2r · Rotta 1913 pp. 7–8

[DI I.3 §1] Because it is self-evident that there is no proportion of the infinite to the finite, from this it is likewise crystal clear that wherever one encounters exceeding and exceeded, one never arrives at the unqualified Maximum, since things that exceed and are exceeded are finite. Such a Maximum, by contrast, is necessarily infinite. Given anything whatsoever that is not the unqualified Maximum itself, it is manifest that a greater can be given. Moreover, because we find equality to exist by degrees—so that one thing is more equal to this than to that according to generic, specific, local, influential, and temporal agreement and difference, along with like conditions—it is plain that neither two nor more things can be found so similar and equal that they could not still be infinitely more similar. Hence measure and thing measured, however equal they may be, will forever remain distinct.

[DI I.3 §2] A finite intellect, therefore, cannot attain the truth of things with precision by means of likeness. For truth is neither more nor less, consisting in a certain indivisible, which nothing that is not the true itself can measure with exactness —just as what is not a circle cannot measure a circle, whose being consists in a certain indivisible. Thus the intellect, which is not truth, never comprehends truth so precisely that it could not be comprehended infinitely more precisely; for it stands to truth as an inscribed polygon stands to a circle: the more angles the inscribed polygon possesses, the more similar to the circle it becomes, yet it is never made equal to the circle, even if one multiplies its angles to infinity, unless it resolves itself into identity with the circle. Clearly, then, concerning the true we know nothing other than that we know it to be incomprehensible precisely as it is—truth standing as most absolute necessity, which can be neither more nor less than it is, and our intellect standing as possibility. Accordingly the quiddity of things, which is the truth of beings, is unattainable in its purity; though sought by every philosopher, by none has it been found as it is; and the more deeply we are schooled in this ignorance, the closer we draw to truth itself.
