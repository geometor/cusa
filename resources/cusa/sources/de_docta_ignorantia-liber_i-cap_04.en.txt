### Book I, Chapter 4 — The Absolute Maximum Is Incomprehensibly Understood, and with It the Minimum Coincides
# Witness: h I, nn. 11–12 · Paris 1514 fol. 2r–2v · Rotta 1913 pp. 9–11

[DI I.4 §1] Since the unqualified and Absolute Maximum, than which nothing can be greater, is greater than can be comprehended by us—because it is infinite truth—we attain it in no other way than incomprehensibly. For inasmuch as it does not belong to the nature of things that admit of exceeding and exceeded, it stands above everything that can be conceived by us; indeed, whatsoever things are apprehended by sense, reason, or intellect differ within themselves and from one another in such wise that there is no precise equality among them. Maximal equality, therefore, which is neither other than nor diverse from anything, surpasses every intellect. Wherefore the Absolute Maximum, since it is everything that can be, is wholly in act; and just as it cannot be greater, by the very same reason it cannot be lesser, since it is everything that can be. Now the Minimum is that than which nothing can be lesser. And because the Maximum is of just this kind, it is manifest that the Minimum coincides with the Maximum.

[DI I.4 §2] This becomes clearer to you if you contract maximum and minimum to quantity. For maximal quantity is maximally large, whereas minimal quantity is maximally small. Absolve, then, maximum and minimum from quantity—subtracting 'large' and 'small' intellectually—and you clearly behold that maximum and minimum coincide; for 'maximum' is a superlative just as 'minimum' is a superlative. Absolute maximality, therefore, is no more maximal than minimal, because within it the minimum is the maximum coincidently.

[DI I.4 §3] Oppositions, accordingly, belong solely to things that admit of exceeding and exceeded, and to these in differing ways; to the Absolute Maximum they belong not at all, because it stands above all opposition. Since, then, the Absolute Maximum is absolutely in act all things that can be, without any opposition whatsoever so that in the Maximum the Minimum coincides, it likewise stands above every affirmation and negation. All that is conceived to be is no more than it is not; and all that is conceived not to be is not any more than it is. Rather, it is 'this' in such a way that it is all things, and all things in such a way that it is none of them; and maximally 'this' in such a way that it is minimally the same. For to say 'God, who is absolute maximality itself, is light' is no other than to say 'God is maximally light in such wise that He is minimally light.' Otherwise absolute maximality would not be all possible things in act, were it not infinite and the bound of all things and boundable by none of them, as by God's own loving-kindness we shall explain in what follows.

[DI I.4 §4] Yet this transcends all our understanding, which cannot combine contradictories in their principle along the pathway of discursive reason, since we walk by way of things made manifest to us by nature; falling far short of this infinite power, reason cannot join together contradictories separated by an infinite distance. Above every discursive movement of reason, therefore, we see incomprehensibly that absolute maximality is infinite, that nothing is opposed to it, and that with it the Minimum coincides. 'Maximum' and 'Minimum,' as taken in this little book, stand as transcendent terms of absolute signification, so that above all contraction to quantity of bulk or of power they embrace all things in their absolute simplicity.
