Mission¶

Nicholas of Cusa as the Philosophical & Projective Hinge of the GEOMETOR Canon

Standing chronologically and conceptually at the watershed between classical-medieval mathematics and early modern projective, infinitesimal, and relativistic thought, Nicholas of Cusa (Nicolaus Cusanus, 1401–1464) transformed geometry from a static science of finite magnitudes into a dynamic art of infinite limits and transsumptive vision.

Within the 27-repository GEOMETOR ecosystem, geometor/cusa bridges four foundational traditions:

  1. From Ancient Exhaustion to Intellectual Limit Vision: Inheriting Plato's Divided Line (geometor/plato), Aristotle's theory of measure and continuum (geometor/aristotle), Euclid's Elements (geometor/euclid & geometor/elements), Archimedes' polygon exhaustion, and Proclus's philosophy of mathematical imagination (geometor/proclus), Cusa distinguishes discursive ratio—which remains bound by non-contradiction among finite magnitudes—from intellectus, which beholds the asymptotic limit where curved and straight coincide.

  2. Projective Continuity & Conformal Circle Geometry: Four centuries before Poncelet’s Principle of Continuity (1822), Möbius circle inversion, and Felix Klein's Erlangen synthesis, De Docta Ignorantia I.13–15 demonstrated that on the completed geometric horizon the straight line is the maximal circle and the maximal degenerate triangle—a truth realized with 100% algebraic precision in geometor.cusa via conformal inversion and Wildberger's Rational Trigonometry (geometor/wildberger).

  3. Constructive Circle Rectification & Fifth-Order Padé Approximation: Across De Geometricis Transmutationibus (1445), De Mathematicis Complementis (1453), De Mathematica Perfectione (1458), and Aurea Propositio in Mathematicis (1459), Cusa pioneered algebraic and geometric conservation laws for rectifying circular arcs—culminating in the three-ray formula \(\theta \approx \frac{3\sin\theta}{2 + \cos\theta}\) later rediscovered by Snellius (1621) and proved optimal by Huygens (1654).

  4. Centerless Cosmology & Perspective Relativity: In De Docta Ignorantia II.11–12, Cusa deduced from the impossibility of exact physical equidistance that the earth moves as a stella nobilis and that every cosmic observer perceives himself at an apparent center—inspiring Kepler (divinus mihi Cusanus, 1596) and modern relativistic cosmology.
