Book I, Chapter 1 — How To Know Is Not-To-Know
----------------------------------------------

*Original GEOMETOR Translation from the Latin · h I, nn. 2–4 · Paris 1514 fol. 1r–1v · Rotta 1913 pp. 2–4*

.. _cusa-en-di-i-1-s-1:

.. folio:: DI I.1 §1

By a divine gift we observe implanted in all things a certain natural desire to exist in the best manner that the condition of each nature permits, and toward this end to act and to possess fitting instruments, within which an innate judgment is suited to the purpose of knowing, lest impulse be in vain and fail to reach rest in the beloved poise of its own nature. Should it perchance turn out otherwise, this must happen by accident, as when illness deceives taste or mere opinion misleads reason. Wherefore we say that a sound and free intellect knows the true—which it insatiably longs to reach by surveying all things through its inborn discursive motion—when it clasps what it has apprehended in a loving embrace, doubting not at all that to be most true from which no sound mind can dissent. Now all who investigate judge what is uncertain proportionally by comparing it with something presupposed as certain; every inquiry, therefore, is comparative and employs the medium of proportion. When the matters investigated can be compared to the presupposition by a close proportional reduction, the judgment of apprehension is easy; when we require many intermediate steps, difficulty and toil arise—just as is familiar in mathematics, where earlier propositions are led back quite easily to the first self-evident principles, whereas later propositions are reduced more arduously because only through the intermediation of the earlier ones.

.. _cusa-en-di-i-1-s-2:

.. folio:: DI I.1 §2

Every inquiry, accordingly, consists in comparative proportion, whether easy or difficult; for which reason the infinite as infinite, since it escapes all proportion, remains unknown. Yet because proportion expresses agreement in some one respect together with otherness, it cannot be understood apart from number. Number therefore encompasses everything capable of proportion. Hence number produces proportion not in quantity alone, but in all things that can in any manner agree and differ, whether substantially or accidentally. Perhaps for this reason Pythagoras judged that all things are constituted and understood through the power of numbers. Nevertheless, exactness of combinations in bodily things and the precise adaptation of the known to the unknown surpass human reason—so much so that it seemed to Socrates that he knew nothing save that he was ignorant, while wisest Solomon declared all things difficult and beyond explanation in speech, and another man of divine spirit said that wisdom and the place of understanding are hidden from the eyes of all the living. If this is so—as even most profound Aristotle affirms in First Philosophy, that in the things most manifest by nature such difficulty befalls us as befalls a night-owl trying to gaze upon the sun—surely, since the desire within us is not in vain, what we desire is to know that we do not know. If we can attain this fully, we shall attain learned ignorance. For nothing more perfect will come even to the man most zealous in learning than to be found most learned in the very ignorance that is proper to him; and anyone will be the more learned the more he knows himself to be ignorant. Toward this end I have undertaken the labor of writing a few words concerning learned ignorance itself.
