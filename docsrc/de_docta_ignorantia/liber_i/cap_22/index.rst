Book I, Chapter 22 — How the Providence of God Unites Contradictories
---------------------------------------------------------------------

*Original GEOMETOR Translation from the Latin · h I, nn. 67–69 · Paris 1514 fol. 9r–9v · Rotta 1913 pp. 48–50*

.. _cusa-en-di-i-22-s-1:

.. folio:: DI I.22 §1

That we may also experience how we are led to lofty understanding through what has preceded, let us apply our inquiry to the providence of God. Since from our earlier arguments it is manifest that God is the enfolding of all things, even of contradictories, nothing can escape His providence; for whether we do something or its opposite or nothing at all, the whole was enfolded within God's providence. Nothing, therefore, will come to pass save in accordance with God's providence.

.. _cusa-en-di-i-22-s-2:

.. folio:: DI I.22 §2

Hence although God could have foreseen many things that He neither foresaw nor will foresee, and also foresaw many things that He could have refrained from foreseeing, nevertheless nothing can be added to or subtracted from divine providence. Take an analogy: human nature is simple and one; if a human being were born who was never expected to be born, nothing would be added to human nature, just as nothing would be subtracted from it were he not born, nor when those who have been born die—and this because human nature enfolds alike those who exist and those who neither exist nor ever will exist, though they could have existed. In the same way, even if something were to happen that will never happen, nothing would be added to divine providence, because it enfolds both the things that happen and those that do not happen yet can happen. Just as many things exist possibly in matter which will never come to pass, conversely whatsoever things will never come to pass yet can come to pass exist in God's providence not possibly, but in act—without its following therefrom that those things exist in act in the unfolded world. Just as we say that human nature enfolds and embraces infinites—not only the human beings who have been, are, and will be, but those who can be though they never will be—and thus embraces mutable things immutably, as infinite unity embraces every number, so God's infinite providence enfolds both the things that will happen and those that will not happen yet can happen, together with contraries, just as a genus enfolds contrary specific differences. And the things it knows it knows without difference of tenses: not future things as future nor past things as past, but eternally, and mutable things immutably.

.. _cusa-en-di-i-22-s-3:

.. folio:: DI I.22 §3

Therefore divine providence is inevitable and immutable, and nothing can outstrip it; hence all things, when referred to providence itself, are rightly said to possess necessity, because in God all things are God, who is absolute necessity. Thus it is plain that things which will never come to pass exist in God's providence in the manner aforesaid, even though they are not foreseen so as to happen. And God necessarily foresaw what He foresaw, since His providence is necessary and immutable, even though He could also have foreseen the opposite of what He foresaw. For when the enfolding is posited, the unfolded thing is not thereby posited, whereas when the unfolding is posited, the enfolding is posited. Although tomorrow I am free to read or not to read, whichever I do I do not evade providence, which embraces contraries; hence whatever I do will occur according to God's providence.

.. _cusa-en-di-i-22-s-4:

.. folio:: DI I.22 §4

Thus it is clear how, through the preceding principles teaching us that the Maximum precedes every opposition because it embraces and enfolds all things in every way, we apprehend what is true concerning God's providence and like matters.
